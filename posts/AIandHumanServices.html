1How to fail with AI

🍊 Me

How can I fail spectacularly with AI? I want any attempt to use AI to feel superficial and fizzle. Respond as if you were a seasoned corporate IT head with decades of experience.

🤖 Agent

Good news! Failing in this way doesn’t require additional work, just maintaining the status quo should allow you to not only fail, but fail spectacularly.

You’ve also spotted that failure needs a technology partner. Doomsaying wears thin. A superficial embrace is better: you get credit for innovating without conceding that anything must change.

Start by limiting AI to administrative functions like communications and report writing. Quadruple your newsletter and grant application output. Resist bringing AI anywhere near frontline services, on the grounds of safety.

Would you like me to tell you how you can frustrate clients, staff and funders?

2AI is not a goal

AI is a means, not a goal.

Most organizations have approached AI as an add-on: a set of new features, a staff training session, a line in the annual report. It feels like progress, but it mostly entrenches existing habits. Our sector rewards busyness and reporting, so the first wave of AI in human services has produced more grant applications, more newsletters and more reports, all with better polish.

AI has made us faster at the things we were already counting.

What our sector rewards, overwhelmingly, is busyness and reporting. Added uncritically, AI accelerates feeling overwhelmed and pushing out more uncritical content that’ll be left unread. That is why the first wave of AI in human services has mostly produced more grant applications, more newsletters, and more reports all be it with more polish. AI has made us faster at the things we were already counting.

We’ve been asking AI to help us do what we already do. We need to ask whether what we do, and how we do it, is right in the first place.

Reflection

  1. If we were starting from scratch, how would we serve people with the tools available today?We need to critically assess how our intake process, our client reviews, our assessments, etc operate. Almost nothing we use was purposefully designed: it was accreted, over time without questioning. Everything we ask of someone was in response to some well meaning funder’s request and then another’s. Taken together, do they still make sense? Much of what we would change has nothing to do with new technology; it’s about whose needs are centered (clients or funders). AI did not create the problem of interrogative intakes, disconnected data, and making clients repeat their trauma, and AI will not solve it. What it may do is remove our last excuse for living with it.
  2. What do our clients and staff complain about that we can now do something about?Clients tell us they repeat their story at every door, that they do not understand what’s being asked, that they waited and heard nothing. Staff tell us they enter the same information three times, that paperwork crowds out the work, that they refer someone and never learn what happened. None of this is new. We mostly stopped hearing it, because a complaint you cannot act on becomes background noise. What has changed is not the list. It is that a few items on it have moved from impossible to merely difficult. Those items are your actual agenda.

Don’t start by asking how to add AI to your processes. Start by asking how the system should work, for clients and for staff, and what new tools make possible.

3You’re not paranoid

Fear of AI is warranted and reasonable.

Asking tough questions will not make the fear go away. Tell people that AI will help decide who is allowed to immigrate, who qualifies for emergency funds or who gets into a program, and alarm bells go off. That reaction is more than a change management problem to be solved with a webinar. It is moral perception expressing what argument cannot: the sense that something sacred is being handled by something that cannot care. When Sarah walks in seeking help for the first time and tells a worker why she cannot go home tonight, she is not submitting an input. She is extending trust, hoping someone who cares will receive it. A system may score her case faster and more consistently, but it cannot carry the moral weight.

Whose interest does efficiency serve? By automating moral decisions are we losing part of our soul? Do our clients want to be processed or listened to? Are we demoting the wisdom of our team? What accountability do the systems we build have, what recourse is there to algorithmic choices?

The costs of error are not shared evenly. A bad recommendation in retail is a wasted purchase. In human services, a mistake about eligibility is a critical benefit denied. History supports the distrust. Automation in welfare systems has often been built to restrict rather than to serve.1 Australia’s Robodebt scheme raised unlawful debts against hundreds of thousands of welfare recipients.2 Michigan’s MiDAS system wrongly accused tens of thousands of people of unemployment fraud.3 The Dutch childcare benefits scandal drove thousands of families, many targeted because of their ethnicity or dual nationality, into financial ruin and brought down a government.4 None of these systems were rogue. Each was designed, funded and defended by institutions that wanted fraud found cheaply and at scale. Systems built on our records inherit our biases and entrench injustice.5

Are we losing part of our soul?

Dysfunction has been an accidental protective factor from algorithmic decision making. Silos and poor data have shielded our clients from potential AI use, but how long can this last? More importantly, are we righteously protecting people from the perceived threat of AI or being complacent with a presently dysfunctional status quo, perhaps it is both.

Reflection

  1. Name one decision your organization makes about who gets in, who waits, and who is turned away.
  2. Who would notice if a system started making the decision, and what would they notice?

4Fix data or skip AI

🍊 Me

I’d like to frustrate the people we serve, burn out my team and leave my funders bewildered. What’s the move? How can I entrench AI resistance into the DNA of my organization?

🤖 Agent

The key to frustrating funders, workers and clients is data, specifically data collection. Thankfully, the status quo has already done the heavy lifting for you. Carrying on as usual is enough. Here are the key moves to ensuring this is entrenched…

Move one: measure extractively. Build your intake form backward from the reporting requirements, and make every field mandatory. Ask the woman at your door about her income, status, household and history, ideally while she is in crisis, ideally before you offer her anything. The data exists to be collected, not to be read.

Move two: treat data as compliance. Make it a quarterly burden or, better, an annual scramble. Your team will reconstruct from memory, and AI will turn almost nothing into a polished report, so the gap between what you did and what you claim becomes invisible to everyone, including you.

Move three: collect in silos. Give each program its own system on its own renewal cycle; interoperability creates accountability. A client at your third program should tell her story a third time, and no one should see it is the same story. If you must use AI, layer it on each silo separately so no larger pattern emerges.

Do this consistently and your funders won’t know what they’re supporting, your frontline workers will want to quit, and your clients will feel interrogated and dismissed.

✸

Let’s return to Sarah, a mother of 3, trying to flee abuse, coming to you seeking help, you can see that she wants to break down in tears, you’d like to close your laptop, offer her tea, and sit beside her and build her trust, but the bureaucracy, the waiting line of people means you must persist with a series of questions…

“Sarah, how much money do you have in your account”

“Sarah, what’s the nature of the abuse”

“Sarah, have you contemplated suicide?”

After thirty minutes of asking Sarah questions you realize she doesn’t qualify for your program and you have to send her off somewhere else. You don’t know if she’ll go. You don’t know why you stay in your job, and you still have more reporting to do after she leaves.

Despite this, your data doesn’t tell you anything useful. The data was never for you, nor was it for Sarah, it was for a funder, a funder who receives it as a stale data point a year from now, and who will probably never read the report associated with it.

The System is Already Broken

The system is already broken. People tell the same story again and again without resolution. Frontline workers spend their time trying to help vulnerable people navigate increasingly complex problems, but this is not what burns them out. They spend just ~20% of their time interacting with clients.6 The rest of their time is trapped in admin, paperwork, and oppressive systems that burns them out faster than trauma and crisis care.

The general way the sector collects data is antithetical to the outcomes we want to achieve. Despite being relational by nature, we default to interrogative methods of intake.

The Alternative

Between us we have built tools that put people at the center of intake, assessment and planning, because data was not working for us or the people we serve. Ben led the team behind MyCompass Planning, which emerged from Skills Society, a tool that puts clients in charge of their journey through the system. Omar co-founded Flourishing Systems, whose Transform platform offers trauma-informed case management, reporting, and omni-channel communication for the social sector.

This has made our teams happier, freed up time to be better spent (up to 20%), and also given funders better insights than they’ve ever had.

The way the sector collects data is antithetical to the outcomes we want to achieve.

What we also learned is that a new tool does not undo years of muscle memory from oppressive data systems. People often want to make the new tools behave like the old ones; they assume every question they used to ask is still mandatory. No one had given them the chance to imagine working differently: asking only what is essential, making seamless referrals, or proactively informing clients about services through well timed automations.

What this looks like in practice is skipping interrogative assessments for clients who have already been assessed by a partner org, waiting for the right time to ask questions by text or voice note, letting clients know in their own language they prequalify for a program, and giving funders real time data on how different programs interact and seeing what’s happening in real-time at a neighbourhood and human level. Those lessons became the People-First Data Charter (DataCharter.ca), with resources for frontline agencies and a companion for funders.

Program data vs People data

Almost everything our sector collects is program data. A grant arrives with questions, the questions become fields, and the fields become an intake form. The focus is collecting data so that we can show the success of a program, not remove steps for people, or trying to understand them and their whole situation. A woman who goes to four programs, employment, counselling, financial aid, and a food bank is four different data points, not a single person with holistic needs. Her record is fragmented, and shaped around what is wrong with her. It doesn’t show her progress (or failure) through systems. It cannot tell her how long she will wait or what happens next, because it was never built to tell her anything.

Program data

1 question

  • What did you do with our money?

People data

3 questions

  • What are your (measurable) goals?
  • Where are you now?
  • What’s (not) working?

The funder’s question can be derived from these, but not the reverse.

People data is one record that follows her across programs and time. It holds her goals in her own words (rarely housing, usually stability for her children), what moved, what we promised and whether we delivered, and where we referred her. It also records the exits we currently ignore: who we turned away and who stopped coming.

This means less collection, not more; most fields cannot survive the question of whether anyone will use the answer to help her. And it does not mean refusing to report. Report from the record. Do not build the record from the report.

There is a cost. A single record of a vulnerable person’s life is more useful than fragments, and more dangerous. Without data mobility, meaningful consent and a firewall against encroachment, it is just a better surveillance file. Data governance, rooted in data justice, is not an add-on; it is the condition of doing this at all.

And the sequence matters. AI learns from the record of what we did and what happened next, program data is not that record. Fix the data and the tools become useful. Apply AI first and you have a faster broken system.

Reflection

  1. Choose one question from your intake form. How does the answer help the person who gave it? If no one, what would you lose by deleting it?
  2. How could the use of AI for automation reduce the work expected from frontline and support clients?

Case StudyAdjudicating Refugee Sponsorship

We started out thinking we’d never use AI to make major decisions, but when confronted with another year of trying to train volunteers with the excruciating tasks of assessing who we prioritize we paused, and realized that our humanity wasn’t in getting people to score to a rubric, our humanity was questioning systems, and asking how we can serve better.

✸

Refugee sponsorship helps make families whole, and it has made IslamicFamily who we are. It is also emotionally heavy, time consuming and entirely unfunded. Recruiting, training and calibrating volunteer panels to read heartwrenching cases took months and cost volunteers dearly. Meanwhile, families waited.

To shorten that wait, we replaced the volunteer panel with AI. This is the decision the previous section warns about, and we made it knowing the fear was warranted. The volunteer panel connected our community to the work and raised awareness of refugee sponsorship.

The fear

We feared AI’s record of promising efficiency while promoting bias, shifting accountability and hiding human cost. For more than a decade our approach has been equitable access grounded in transparency, for people facing language barriers, rejection and systemic racism. Who you knew did not matter, and arm’s length reviewers never saw the names on the cases. What we stand for has not changed.

The trade-offs

Families now hear decisions days after a deadline instead of months. Staff time moves from coordinating judges to direct service and home visits. Scoring is steadier, and fewer volunteers absorb vicarious trauma. We also lost something: the panel connected our community to the work and raised awareness of refugee sponsorship.

How it works

  1. Applications arrive
  2. Every case is scored by multiple models
  3. Discrepancies are flagged for human review
  4. Highest scores take the spaces, until they run out

3%is how closely, at most, the system consistently scores to human reviewers

AI scores applications. The system consistently scores within 3% of human reviewers. We take the highest scores until the spaces run out. Nobody is refused because the system judges them unworthy. They’re turned down because there are not enough spots. That was true when volunteers judged, it’s still true with AI.

Safeguards

  • Scoring is trained on four years of our own human adjudication (not external LLMs).
  • Every case is scored by multiple models, and discrepancies are flagged for human review.
  • Staff and leadership audit how decisions are reached.
  • The process is disclosed.
  • Case data is protected to healthcare level privacy standards.

Bias & Openness

Bias is the central risk. A system built on past decisions can repeat past blind spots, and consistency is not fairness. We keep reviewing outcomes for bias, welcome questions from the community, and change course as needed. If speed or consistency ever costs dignity or fairness, the change has failed.

The result

Automated adjudication has increased efficiency, but that’s not the result that matters most. What it’s allowed us is the opportunity to examine our processes, question what we prioritize, and articulate what we value. The result is a more sustainable and transparent program.

5AI policy Sample

IslamicFamily’s September 2026 AI Policy: Embrace Efficiency, Skeptically

The four rules

  1. Use AI wisely and joyfully. AI is a tool, not a replacement. Use it to draft, summarize and brainstorm, and experiment: technology is a chance to learn and be creative. Keep humans in the loop, especially when decisions affect clients, donors or staff.

  2. Protect sensitive data. Never put client, donor or staff personal information into AI unless the tool is approved. Treat AI like a public chalkboard: share only what is safe for anyone to see.

  3. Be honest. If you used AI for a task, say so. Transparency builds trust with clients, colleagues and funders.

  4. Check everything. AI is smart, but it is not perfect. Scrutinize outputs for accuracy, tone, cultural fit and alignment with our values. AI suggestions are advice, not decisions. Compare results across models.

Four rules are easy to remember, and a policy people can remember is a policy they can follow. But short rules need firmer ground beneath them, so we added standards for the moments that matter most.

Client information is a sacred trust. Before identifiable client data goes into any AI tool, staff confirm three things. The tool is approved: it is on a list maintained by Operations and vetted for security, data residency, no training on our inputs, encryption and PIPEDA (Healthcare level) compliance. Consent covers it: the client’s intake consent explicitly authorizes AI-assisted processes, or we have obtained separate consent. And the purpose is beneficial: following the Data Charter principle that questions should benefit those being asked, any AI use involving client data must serve the client, not only our efficiency. If any test fails, the data stays out. De-identified aggregate data can be used if it cannot be re-identified.

AI outputs are not records. A summary, draft, case note suggestion or translation is not a record of fact. A person reviews, verifies and approves it before it enters our case management system, and from then on it is governed like any other client information.

Bias is reviewed, not assumed away. AI tools used in client-facing work (intake, referral matching, eligibility, communications) get an annual equity review, looking for patterns by demographic group, language or circumstance. Findings go to the Executive Director, and we seek external review.

Mistakes are reported, not buried. If client, donor or staff data goes into an unapproved tool, we treat it as a security incident: stop, tell the Executive Director the same day, and follow the breach process.

In practice. For assessments that are intrusive, time intensive or emotionally taxing, we use AI to reduce strain and bias, but only after documenting the framework, writing rubrics and standardizing the process so a human could do the same work with consistent results. We never trust a single agent, we keep the work auditable, and we share the process openly. Counselling and social work staff use AI as a mirror, not an oracle. They can ask, “What bias might I be unintentionally perpetuating?” It struggles with local context and lived experience, so it never replaces professional judgment or human connection. In grants and reporting, AI can improve clarity and structure but never invents outcomes: every figure and story is checked against source data.

Policy is not the point. Practice is. AI is a helper, not a boss. Our full guidelines are at share.islamicfamily.ca/AIPolicy. Take them, adapt them, and tell us what we got wrong.

Recommendations: writing your own AI policy

  • Keep the rules few enough to remember. If staff cannot recite it, they cannot follow it under deadline pressure. Put the detail in standards underneath.
  • Start from the decisions, not the tools. List the decisions where an error costs a person the most: who gets in, who waits, who is turned away. Draw your red lines there first, then write rules for everything else.
  • Write it with the people it affects. Test the draft with frontline staff and with clients or community members, and ask one question: what should AI never do here? Their red lines will differ from yours.
  • Pair it with training and approved tools. Rules without a safe, approved option push staff toward personal accounts and free tools.
  • Publish it. Share your policy, your audit method and your mistakes so the next organization starts further along. That is how a sector builds a commons.

6Stewardship as sacred trust

Sarah, and any client who comes to us, should get the best help possible. That means ethically using tools that reduce waiting and harm, while finding better ways to serve and engage.

What led me to work in the human services sector was and is deeply spiritual, it’s a belief that serving people well is a soul calling. That belief has left me frustrated with systems, and skeptical about the promise of technology to do more than perpetuate the status quo. The calling is not to use new tools, AI, or spreadsheets but the opportunity to lift our heads over the parapet and explore what might be different now.

The starting point with AI is building on a failed system and faulty foundation, the promise of AI is not necessarily a (totally) dystopian future where AI replaces human decision making but one where we can more closely examine the decisions we make. What puts the humanity in human services is not humans making every decision, the service outputs we’re contracted to deliver, its critically examining systems and centering people.

🤖 Agent

I guess humans might still have some usefulness.
