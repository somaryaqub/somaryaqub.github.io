When Secularism Becomes AssimilationThe Residential School Mentality in Contemporary Québec

Mirza-Zain Baig · November 7, 2026

▶  Listen

Have you ever considered that a society may sincerely condemn an injustice of its past while reproducing the very assumptions that made it possible? For the greater part of two decades, Canada has confronted the residential school system through public apologies, inquiries, commemorations, and the work of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Canadians have been asked to recognise the devastating consequences of a government that believed it understood better than families and communities how children ought to be raised and culturally formed. The principle that the state must not override a child’s cultural and spiritual inheritance has thus acquired, at least rhetorically, the status of a settled conviction.

It is against this backdrop that Québec’s expanding secularism laws acquire their significance. Since 2019, the province has progressively extended the reach of its secularism legislation, and the newly elected government now proposes to prohibit elementary school students from wearing visible religious symbols. The question this raises is uncomfortable but unavoidable. Has Canadian society genuinely rejected the mentality that made residential schools possible, or has it merely altered the ideology that the state seeks to impose? That mentality did not disappear with the closure of the last school in 1996. It changed its vocabulary, and the language of civilisation is readily exchanged for the language of religious neutrality.

To pose this question is not to suggest that Québec’s secularism laws are identical to the residential school system. They are not. That system involved the forced separation of children from their families, the systematic destruction of Indigenous cultures, widespread neglect and abuse, and the deaths of thousands of children. History, however, is not understood solely through the comparison of outcomes. It must also be examined through the assumptions and philosophies that made those outcomes conceivable, and it is at this level that Québec’s approach to religious expression deserves scrutiny.

The Premise Behind the Residential School System

At the heart of the residential school system lay a paternalistic conviction that the state, acting with religious institutions, possessed the moral authority to determine which cultural and spiritual identities children ought to inherit. Indigenous children were removed from their families, forbidden or discouraged from speaking their languages, and placed in a system designed to sever them from their ancestral traditions. The objective was never literacy or vocational skill alone. It was the transformation of the children themselves, which the Truth and Reconciliation Commission rightly characterised as cultural genocide. What made the system insidious was that its architects spoke the language of improvement, civilisation, and education. The suppression of Indigenous identity was presented as a step toward a better future, and the vocabulary of benevolence concealed the coercion it accompanied. The essential defect, therefore, lay not only in the system’s methods but in its premise that a child’s inheritance could legitimately be overridden by the state’s preferred vision of society. That premise ought to concern us wherever it reappears, whatever ideology carries it.

It may be objected that no child in Québec today is being taken from the family home, and that this alone places the present debate beyond comparison. The objection mistakes the instrument for the objective. The removal of Indigenous children was the method, and their forced assimilation was the purpose, achieved in large part by alienating them from the culture, language, and faith of their parents. The absence of removal does not, therefore, establish the absence of alienation. A child may sleep at home every night and still spend most of every school day in an institution that teaches, through its rules if not its words, that what the child carries from home has no place in public life. Alienation does not require a dormitory. It requires only an institution with authority over a child’s formative years and a settled conviction that the child’s inheritance is something to be outgrown.

This paper does not claim that Québec intends to assimilate anyone. An intention to assimilate and a policy that produces assimilative pressure are distinct phenomena that ought not to be conflated. For present purposes, assimilative pressure refers to institutional conditions that encourage individuals to conceal or abandon the visible expression of their identity as the price of participation in public life. Such consequences matter even where intentions are benign, and the architects of the residential school system furnish perhaps the clearest evidence that a sincere belief in one’s own benevolence offers no safeguard against them.

Secularism and Its Expansion in Québec

There is an undeniable irony in Québec’s present trajectory. For generations, the Catholic Church dominated the province’s schools, social institutions, and politics, and the Quiet Revolution allowed Québec to reclaim its public institutions from clerical control. Secularism, understood as the independence of government from religious authority, emerged as an important safeguard of collective freedom, and many Quebecers rightly regard it as an achievement worth defending. Yet an essential distinction must be preserved. Freeing the state from religious control is not the same as freeing society from the visible presence of religion. A government may be religiously neutral while serving deeply religious citizens, and a teacher may meet every professional standard while wearing a hijab, a turban, or a kippah. The neutrality of the institution is preserved precisely because it is the institution, and not the individual, that is required to be neutral.

Québec’s legislation has steadily eroded this distinction. Bill 21, adopted in 2019, prohibited teachers and certain other public employees from wearing religious symbols at work. A ministerial directive issued in 2023 prohibited schools from setting aside space for prayer, and Bill 94, adopted in October 2025, codified that prohibition, extended the ban on religious symbols to all school staff and volunteers, and required students to keep their faces uncovered. Further legislation sanctioned in April 2026 carried the regime into daycares, subsidised private schools, CEGEPs, and universities, with provisions on religious practice taking effect on September 1. The Parti Québécois government now intends to extend the restrictions to elementary school students, and Education Minister Joël Arseneau has described the proposal as not open to negotiation. As of November 2026, no bill has been tabled, a minority government will require the support of another party to enact one, and the measure remains a proposal rather than law.

It is worth observing, moreover, that the very principle in whose name these measures are adopted has never been clearly defined. Within the past decade, three different parties have governed Québec, the Liberals, the Coalition Avenir Québec, and now the Parti Québécois, and the meaning of laïcité has shifted with each of them. The Liberal government’s 2017 law spoke of the religious neutrality of the state and required faces to be uncovered when giving or receiving public services, the Coalition Avenir Québec turned to religious symbols worn by public employees, and the Parti Québécois now proposes to reach the students themselves. Each new measure has been justified on the ground that the previous one did not go far enough. It is telling that the present proposal has been rejected even by the architects of Bill 21. In 2025, Simon Jolin-Barrette, the minister who carried that law, accused the Parti Québécois of attacking children by proposing to ban religious symbols for elementary students. When the author of the province’s central secularism law regards its next extension as excessive, the claim that it merely completes an unfinished principle becomes difficult to sustain.

The same uncertainty surfaces in the classroom. One of my professors at the Université du Québec à Montréal, in one session, remarked that many religious minorities do not share the laïque values of Québec society. The remark invites an obvious question. What, precisely, are those values? Was Québec not already laïque in 2017, or in 2019? If it was, what does it now mean to strengthen laïcité, and by what measure has the province not yet gone far enough? The separation of the state from religious authority is a principle that most Quebecers understand and accept, religious minorities among them. The present push, however, extends well beyond that separation, toward the regulation of how individuals may appear and practise within public institutions. A principle whose boundaries move with every government offers citizens no stable assurance of where the state’s authority ends, and it allows any visible minority to be described, at any moment, as falling short of values that have never been fixed.

It is fair, then, to ask what is actually being addressed. One need not attribute motives to anyone to observe the pattern. A principle that is never satisfied, and that falls almost entirely on those whose faith is visible, begins to resemble something other than a principle. When the stated justification shifts from neutrality to conscience to the protection of children, while the people affected remain the same, the possibility must at least be considered that the real object of these measures is not religion in the abstract but a discomfort with certain kinds of visible difference, and that laïcité has become the language in which that discomfort is permitted to speak.

The Notwithstanding Clause

These measures are also shielded from ordinary judicial scrutiny. Bill 21 invokes section 33 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, the notwithstanding clause, which permits a legislature to override specified guarantees, among them freedom of religion and equality rights. The Supreme Court of Canada heard arguments in 2026 on the extent of that power and has not yet delivered judgment. For present purposes, its significance lies in what it would mean for children. Should the prohibition be extended to students and shielded in the same manner, the religious freedom of six year olds would be limited not because a court had weighed the limit and found it justified, but because the legislature had declared in advance that the question shall not be asked. A child would thus lose a fundamental freedom without anyone ever being required to demonstrate why. Legality and legitimacy are not the same thing. Much of the residential school system operated under the authority of Canadian law, a reminder that legal authorisation does not, by itself, establish moral legitimacy.

The notwithstanding clause also exposes the weakness of a further justification, namely that minorities must bend to the will of the majority. Which majority is meant? It may be answered that education falls within provincial jurisdiction, so that the relevant majority is Québec’s, and that Montreal, however different its outlook, is simply a minority within the province. Yet the same logic applies one level higher. The rights at stake are guaranteed by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, and within the federation that adopted it, it is Québec that constitutes the minority. Québec has not reconciled its secularism laws with those guarantees. It has shielded the laws from them altogether. When Québec’s Superior Court reviewed Bill 21 in 2021, it described the law’s conflict with protected rights as incontestable, and upheld it because of that clause. A province that insists on its majority when governing its schools, while setting aside the constitutional protections of the larger federation in which it is itself a minority, cannot coherently tell its own religious minorities that the will of the majority must prevail. The argument from majority, applied consistently, either dissolves Québec’s own claim to distinctiveness or extends that same protection to the minorities whose practices are now restricted.

From the Neutrality of the State to the Neutrality of the Individual

The shift these measures reveal is unmistakable. Québec has moved from the neutrality of the state to the neutrality of the individual. A child is not an agent of the state, a child wearing a religious symbol exercises no governmental authority, and a visibly religious student does not make a school religious. If the state cannot distinguish its own neutrality from the personal identity of those it serves, secularism ceases to guarantee freedom and begins to demand conformity. The reasoning offered for this shift does not withstand examination. It is often said that a person praying quietly in a corner of a public building makes others uncomfortable, and that religious practice belongs in private. But discomfort is not harm, and a society that legislated against every sight that unsettled some of its members would leave little of public life intact. Public space belongs to every citizen, the devout and the indifferent alike. Nor is the principle applied consistently, since citizens are not required to conceal their political convictions or cultural dress. Religious conviction alone is singled out as too private to be seen, and a rule that applies to one category of expression alone is not neutrality. It is a preference given the force of law.

A Personal Account

This shift is not abstract to me. I trained as a teacher while Bill 21 was being implemented. I wore traditional Muslim clothing throughout my internships. It was not a strict religious obligation for me. What I resisted was the suggestion that removing it was necessary to demonstrate professional impartiality. Bill 21 governed teachers employed by school boards, and an intern remained a university student, so I was acting within the law. Even so, I lost three prospective internship placements. For two of them, I could only infer that the schools did not want the controversy my appearance might bring. For the third, nothing was left to inference. My program wrote to tell me that my assigned cooperating teacher had decided not to continue with the placement “due to the religious attire you wear.” My visit to the school, the message explained, had created significant turmoil, and her colleagues had given her a hard time afterwards. She was, in the program’s words, devastated. The placement ended not because of anything I did in a classroom, but because of how others reacted to how I looked.

When I eventually secured a placement, I experienced a sense of alienation in the staffroom. I was reminded more than once that my attire could keep me out of the profession, and those reminders were not baseless, since Bill 21 would have barred me from wearing it once hired. I was cautioned against speaking about religion, even when asked about my own clothing. I found myself repeatedly required to explain my appearance, in a setting where I believed I was fully compliant with the law.

If I was acting within the law, the question arises as to what, precisely, the difficulty was. An internship exists to assess a future teacher against Québec’s professional competencies, among them the ability to plan and deliver instruction, manage a classroom, respect the diversity of students, and act with professional and ethical judgement. One of my evaluations addressed my appearance directly. “While Mirza wears traditional Muslim clothing, he maintains a professional image.” It affirmed my professionalism. But the word “while” sets the clothing and the professional image against each other, as though one had to be reconciled with the other. It is not evident why religious dress should require reconciliation with professionalism at all. The sentence proves nothing about discrimination. It shows only how naturally a visible faith becomes a separate consideration, even in an assessment that ends well.

A diploma does not guarantee good character. A visible religious identity is no evidence of partiality, any more than a bare head is evidence of fairness. Had I removed my attire each morning before entering the school, I would have entered the same classroom with the same convictions and the same understanding of the world. Every teacher carries a faith, a politics or a philosophy into the room. What the profession asks is that none of it be imposed on students. Neutrality is not the absence of belief. It is the capacity to act fairly despite possessing beliefs, and that capacity can only be judged by conduct.

Those months demonstrated that a law and the culture that develops around it are different things. Being compliant, being competent and being seen as acceptable were three separate tests. I believed that I satisfied the first two. It was the third that proved decisive. Not every school behaves this way. But when neutrality is measured by appearance, lawful religious expression becomes professionally suspect, and institutions learn to avoid the believer rather than evaluate the teacher. Silencing an expression does not extinguish the conviction behind it. It merely allows society to mistake invisibility for neutrality.

If an adult, trained and formally assessed, could be met with such suspicion, the consequences of extending the same reasoning to a seven year old warrant careful consideration. A teacher at least holds a position of public authority, and Québec may advance an argument, however contestable, for regulating the manner in which its employees present themselves. A student, by contrast, represents no one. Whatever view one takes of Bill 21, restrictions applicable to employees cannot simply be transferred to children without an independent justification.

Who Decides a Child’s Formation?

The deepest parallel with the residential school system therefore lies in a question of authority. Who possesses the right to determine the cultural and spiritual formation of a child? Under the residential school system, governments and churches claimed that right over Indigenous families. Today, the government asserts its own authority over the same question, in the name of protecting children from religious pressure. A categorical prohibition, however, presumes that a religious symbol signifies oppression even where the child and family understand it as faith and belonging. Consider a young Sikh boy who wears a patka, a Jewish boy who wears a kippah, or a Muslim girl who wears a hijab. Some may be following family expectations, while others embrace traditions that give them community and purpose. A blanket restriction does not inquire. It tells all of them that they are welcome at school provided that they leave a visible part of themselves outside.

A familiar rejoinder holds that a woman who will not remove her hijab has simply made a choice. When daycare educators began losing their positions under Bill 94 in February 2026, former education minister Bernard Drainville, who had tabled the bill, put it in precisely those terms. “They could have very well decided to take off their religious symbols during working hours,” he said. “They decided not to do that. They decided not to respect the law. And therefore, it’s their decision and unfortunately, they have to bear the consequences of their own personal choice.” One of the educators affected described the same events in very different words. “We were forced to leave our jobs. It was not our choice.”

The argument appears reasonable only until it is examined. To call the removal of the hijab a simple choice presupposes that the hijab carries no real weight, that it is an accessory a sensible person would set aside without loss. This presupposition is not new. Those who designed the residential school system regarded Indigenous beliefs as the superstitions of savages and Indigenous languages as uncivilised speech. From that vantage point, leaving them behind was no sacrifice, and resistance could be dismissed as stubbornness. The same reasoning now attends the hijab. Once it is cast as backward, its removal is made to appear effortless. Yet for many women the very idea of removing it in public is traumatising, let alone being told to do so in order to keep a job or remain present in a child’s education. A demand does not cease to be coercive because the person subjected to it is technically free to refuse and bear the consequences.

Answering the Case for the Prohibition

The supporters of Québec’s approach raise concerns that deserve an answer. They argue that children may face religious pressure from parents or communities, that schools should be free from proselytism, and that the state must protect freedom of conscience, including the freedom not to believe. Québec’s history with the Catholic Church explains why many citizens regard institutional secularism as requiring active defence. The question is whether the measures actually distinguish between protecting freedom and restricting it. Preventing a teacher from coercing students into religious observance is one matter, and prohibiting a child from voluntarily displaying a religious identity is quite another. The former protects the child against the imposition of belief, whereas the latter restricts the child’s own expression of it.

Coercion within some families, however, does not establish coercion within every family, and a religious symbol is not, in itself, evidence that a child is being coerced. The protection of one child from being compelled to practise a faith ought not to require the prohibition of another child’s willing practice. Freedom of conscience has always operated in both directions, protecting individuals from compulsion and protecting their right to profess their beliefs openly. A blanket prohibition honours the first of these protections and abandons the second, treating a coerced child and a devout child as though they were the same. To this effect, where the government’s genuine concern is that children are being compelled into religious observance, that concern is legitimate and calls for policies capable of identifying and addressing such compulsion where it in fact exists. The protection of freedom of conscience requires a distinction between coercion and conviction, and a universal prohibition declines to draw it. It does not make the child freer. It merely transfers the authority to decide acceptable religious expression from one institution to another.

A more subtle form of the argument concerns the influence of other children. A girl, it is suggested, may feel pressured to wear the hijab because her classmates do. Peer pressure is real, but it operates in every direction. A prohibition does not remove the pressure to conform. It determines, on the authority of the state, the direction in which that pressure will be exerted, and it communicates to every child in the classroom that visible faith is something to be avoided. To answer the pressure that some children may experience by prohibiting the faith of all of them is to confuse the remedy with the problem.

The most serious argument in favour of the prohibition, and the one most often heard in Québec, concerns the equality of women. The hijab, it is said, symbolises the subordination of women, and a society committed to gender equality cannot accommodate it in its schools. The equality of women means, at its core, that women decide for themselves how they live, worship, and dress. A law that forbids a woman to wear the hijab does not liberate her from the dictation of others. It substitutes the state’s dictation for whatever pressure the law presumes she faces, and it does so without ever asking her. Where she declines to comply, the practical effect is to exclude her from a classroom, a profession, or her children’s school life, which narrows her independence rather than enlarging it. Nor can gender equality explain a prohibition that applies equally to the kippah of a Jewish boy and the patka of a Sikh boy. It is answered, finally, that the law treats all religions alike. Yet a rule that forbids everyone from wearing what only some are religiously obliged to wear is equal in form alone. As Anatole France observed, the law in its majestic equality forbids the rich as well as the poor to sleep under bridges. Its burden falls almost entirely upon the faiths whose observance is visible, and no formal symmetry alters that reality.

What Is the Prohibition For?

It is worth asking, then, what the prohibition is actually for. If the purpose is state neutrality, the measure fails on its own terms, for the reasons already given. If the purpose is meaningful consent, on the ground that a young child cannot freely choose to wear a religious symbol, the argument opens a conundrum the government has not addressed. What becomes of the girl who says, plainly and sincerely, that she wishes to wear the hijab, or the boy who asks to wear his patka because his father and grandfather wear one? Is the child’s wish to be disregarded precisely because it is religious? And if consent is the governing principle, how far is the state prepared to police the choices parents make for their children? A child of six does not consent to the language he is raised in, his name, his food, his school, or the values taught at the dinner table. Each is a parental choice made for a child not yet capable of meaningful consent, and each shapes identity far more profoundly than a piece of cloth. The state does not interrogate any of them. That it singles out religion alone suggests that the concern is not consent at all, but the content of what is being transmitted.

Nor is the restriction answered by the assurance that children remain free to wear religious symbols outside school. The arithmetic of a child’s day tells a different story. Consider a child awake for roughly fourteen hours, who spends about seven at school and a further two travelling to and from it. A child does not dress twice in a morning, once for the journey and again at the school door, so the restriction in practice governs the day from departure to return. That is nine of fourteen waking hours, nearly two thirds of the child’s conscious day, and much of what remains is consumed by meals, homework, and bedtime. Across a week, it reaches some forty five of roughly ninety eight waking hours, close to half of the child’s waking life, and it falls on precisely the hours when the child is most visible to peers and most actively forming a sense of self. Permission to wear the symbol in the evenings and at weekends restores nothing. It confirms that the child’s faith is tolerated only when the public is not looking.

The importance of those hours is not incidental. Québec’s own education regulations define the purpose of schooling in terms of integration. The Régime pédagogique states that preschool services aim to foster the développement intégral of the student and to allow the child to integrate gradually into society, that primary education aims at the student’s insertion dans la société, and that secondary education continues to favour the student’s insertion sociale. During the pandemic, Québec imposed some of the strictest public health measures in the country, among them a curfew and a vaccine passport, yet in May 2020 it was the first province to reopen its elementary schools. Premier Legault stated plainly that the schools were reopening for social reasons. School, in other words, is understood by the province itself as the principal instrument through which children are brought into society. Integration is a legitimate aim, and no one disputes that every child should learn French, understand democratic institutions, and take a full part in Québec’s common life. But when participation in that common life is made conditional upon concealing a visible part of one’s identity, integration ceases to be integration. It becomes assimilation under a gentler name, and the institution designed to bring children into society becomes the means by which they are asked to leave part of themselves behind.

A further inconsistency emerges when religion is set beside other dimensions of identity. Québec and Canada have, in recent decades, firmly accepted that LGBT people must not be compelled to conceal who they are or whom they love in order to participate in public life. That position rests on a principle, namely that a core dimension of a person’s identity is not something the state may require them to hide. For a great many people, religion and spirituality constitute exactly such a dimension, and for some they stand above every other attachment, forming the framework within which family, love, and meaning are understood. A society that accepts the principle for one deeply held identity while insisting that another be removed at the classroom door is not neutral. It has ranked the identities of its citizens and decided which of them are worthy of public expression.

Conclusion

This brings the argument back to the lesson Canada ought to draw from its history. That lesson is not that religion should be excluded from education. The residential school system was not unjust merely because churches participated in it. It was destructive because governments and institutions together denied Indigenous communities the right to preserve and transmit their languages, cultures, and spiritual traditions. To conclude that the state should now possess greater authority to suppress religious expression is to misunderstand the injustice altogether. The relevant lesson concerns the limits of institutional power. There is, accordingly, a troubling contradiction in a society that commemorates Indigenous children whose identities were suppressed while debating whether other children may express their faith at school. The circumstances differ immensely, since Indigenous peoples endured a colonial project of dispossession and forced separation that no religious minority in Québec is undergoing. But that distinction does not prevent us from naming a recurring danger, namely the belief that the state may define which identities fit its conception of the ideal citizen. History need not repeat itself exactly for its assumptions to warrant examination.

Québec’s debate over secularism concerns more than religious dress or school regulations. It concerns whether public institutions will remain neutral while citizens retain their differences, or whether neutrality will become a condition imposed upon individuals themselves. The comparison with residential schools serves to prompt reflection on the dangers of governments exercising excessive authority over children’s identities, not to diminish Indigenous suffering. The state is obliged to educate children, protect them from coercion, and prepare them for democratic life. It does not follow that it must erase or conceal their spiritual and cultural inheritance to do so, and Canada’s history offers a powerful warning of what follows when institutions mistake assimilation for progress and paternalism for protection.

To conclude, the question has never been whether Québec’s secularism laws are identical to the residential school system, for they are not. It is whether the same assumptions about state authority, conformity, and the necessity of suppressing difference are resurfacing under a new philosophical justification. A society shows that it has learned from its history not merely by condemning past injustices, but by interrogating the habits of thought that produced them. Perhaps the most difficult lesson is that freedom does not consist in replacing one compulsory worldview with another. It consists in recognising the limits of the state’s authority to decide what its citizens, and above all its children, must believe, practise, or visibly become. It begins where the state accepts that a child’s identity was never its to define.
