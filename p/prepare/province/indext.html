Building the community-anchor layer of Edmonton's emergency social services: a coordinated preparedness proposal
A proposal for coordinated response

We can shelter thousands in a single night. We must also reliably connect people and families to the communities that will carry them through the years that follow.

Edmonton Fire Rescue Services and IslamicFamily propose a coordinated, EFRS-led function that brings cultural agencies, faith communities, and social service organizations into emergency preparedness before the next disaster, not after it.

Edmonton is already a reception city, again and again.
2,813
Syrian refugees resettled in Edmonton since 2016, most within a compressed window.
~31,000
Ukrainian evacuees registered across Alberta by 2023, in 194 communities.
~88,000
People evacuated in the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire, with Edmonton a primary host city.

Each wave was met with real goodwill, and coordinated, each time, largely from scratch.

01

Displacement now arrives from every direction

Edmonton receives people fleeing disaster from within our own province and from around the world. Wildfire and flood evacuations from Fort McMurray, Grande Prairie, and northern communities. Families arriving from conflict in Ukraine, in Gaza, and elsewhere. The trigger differs, but the arrival looks much the same: people land in our city with almost nothing, and they land quickly.

Edmonton Fire Rescue Services and its partners are good at the first hours. Shelter, registration, food, immediate safety: these are well drilled and reliably delivered. The problem begins the moment the immediate emergency ends and the longer work of resettlement begins. That is the stretch where the system frays, and it is the stretch this proposal is written to fix.

02

What we see on the ground

Support for evacuees and the civil society response around them is not coordinated. Community organizations do not know what their role is, or how they can help, while the agencies already doing the work are overwhelmed and under-resourced. Multiple organizations independently launch the same clothing drive while no one owns systems navigation. Mental health support arrives as a single model that does not fit the languages, faiths, and histories of the people in front of us.

This is not a new observation. In 2019 the Edmonton Chamber of Voluntary Organizations studied Edmonton's Syrian response and captured a lament heard again and again from those involved: that each time, they were "inventing systems as we went along." The result is a response that is simultaneously duplicated and full of holes. We are not short on goodwill or capacity in this city. We are short on a plan that connects them.

03

Three gaps we can name

G1

Mental health support is one size fits all

A single delivery model is offered to everyone, regardless of language, faith, culture, or the specific trauma people carry. The 2019 Edmonton study found needs going unmet for want of specialists, particularly in Arabic. Many evacuees never receive support that fits them, so they go without.

G2

Community connection is unmapped and disorganized

There is no shared map of which activities, foods, programs, and services exist for which communities. People are left to find their own way to belonging, at exactly the moment they have the least capacity to do so.

G3

Financial and crisis supports are poorly organized

Several organizations pile onto the same visible needs (clothing, for example) while critical functions like systems navigation and financial crisis support have no clear owner at all.

04

The opportunity

Every one of these gaps is a coordination problem, not a capacity problem. Edmonton already has the organizations, the trust, and the reach. Bringing them into the plan before the next disaster turns scattered effort into a system.

05

A second dividend: stronger EFRS, stronger civil society

The case for this stands on evacuee outcomes alone. But a coordinated model pays a second dividend, one that accrues to Edmonton Fire Rescue Services and to the city's civil society, and that lasts between emergencies.

  • Trust and rapport in every community

    Working shoulder to shoulder with cultural, faith, and settlement organizations builds relationships that serve EFRS well beyond disasters, in fire safety, prevention, and everyday community risk work, and earns trust in communities that first responders can otherwise find hard to reach.

  • A broader recruiting pool

    Deeper community relationships widen the pipeline of people who can see themselves in the fire service, helping EFRS recruit and better reflect the city it protects.

  • A stronger, more resilient civil society

    Resourcing and connecting community organizations leaves the whole sector more capable, better coordinated, and more resilient: an asset the city can draw on in every kind of crisis, not only evacuations.

06

This is proven practice, not an experiment

Formally organizing civil society into emergency response is well established in comparable jurisdictions. Two mature models show both the shape and the payoff.

United States · 50+ years

National VOAD

Founded after Hurricane Camille in 1969, when independent responders left survivors helped haphazardly. Today it spans 70+ national organizations, 56 state and territorial bodies, and local chapters that deliberately include groups not usually seen as disaster responders. FEMA formalizes the link through Voluntary Agency Liaisons.

United Kingdom · government-funded

VCS Emergencies Partnership

Formed after the Grenfell fire and the 2017 crises, it now unites 250+ organizations, is co-chaired by the British Red Cross, and explicitly includes faith and equality organizations alongside statutory services. The UK government funded its build-out, and the sector is campaigning to enshrine its role in law.

Closer to home, Alberta already has the foundations through AEMA's Emergency Social Services Framework and the NGO Council of Alberta. What is missing here is the community-anchor layer. This proposal builds it.

07

Building on Alberta's Emergency Social Services Framework

This proposal is not a new priority for the Province. It operationalizes one Alberta has already set. The 2025 Emergency Social Services (ESS) Framework, administered by AEMA under the Emergency Management Act, makes municipalities responsible for ensuring evacuees can access a defined set of services. Among the required "personal services," the framework already names multicultural and linguistic services, and mental health and psychosocial supports.

In other words, the Province has already committed, on paper, to the very supports that gaps G1 and G2 describe. What the framework does not do is say who delivers culturally-specific support, or how, or resource the community anchors who actually can. It names the requirement. This proposal supplies the delivery mechanism.

The framework also gives EFRS its role. Its "host community" provision covers exactly Edmonton's situation: when an evacuation sends people into another jurisdiction, the receiving community provides their emergency social services. Edmonton is perpetually that host, for Fort McMurray, for northern wildfire evacuations, for international arrivals. A standing, EFRS-led coordination function is, in the Province's own language, Edmonton's host-community ESS capability, pre-arranged and community-anchored rather than improvised each time. And because ESS is meant to be planned in advance through written plans and pre-completed service agreements, this proposal is simply that planning done properly: the cultural, faith, and settlement anchors wired into the plan before activation. In doing so, it also advances the ministry's own mandate to keep Alberta prepared for and resilient to the impacts of disasters: community resilience made concrete, built in the places people actually turn to.

The framework already requires it. This proposal delivers it.
08

What we propose to build

An EFRS-led community coordination function for emergency preparedness and evacuee response, designed with civil society from the start rather than consulted at the end. Edmonton Fire Rescue Services convenes and leads. Two anchor partner agencies receive dedicated, embedded staffing so the work is shared and grounded in community, not layered onto already stretched volunteers.

EFRS leads and convenes

The coordination function is housed within EFRS, giving it clear authority, a permanent home, and a direct line into the City and Provincial emergency management structure, including AEMA and the Emergency Social Services system.

A standing seat for civil society

Cultural agencies, faith communities, and social service organizations hold a formal, standing seat in emergency preparedness planning, so the people evacuees already trust are in the room before the next disaster, not phoned during it.

09

Why IslamicFamily is the right anchor partner

A coordinated response needs an anchor that communities already trust across many origins, not one. IslamicFamily has played this role in Edmonton for nearly four decades, most often without any dedicated resourcing. It is no coincidence that when the 2019 study documented Edmonton's welcome of Syrian refugees, its photographs were of IslamicFamily meeting new Edmontonians at the airport.

  • Trusted across many communities, not one

    IslamicFamily's reach spans dozens of cultural and linguistic communities. For families arriving in crisis, it is a door that already feels familiar, regardless of where in the world they have come from.

  • On the frontline of international crises that land in Edmonton

    When displacement from Syria, Afghanistan, Ukraine, Sudan, Gaza, and other crises reaches our city, IslamicFamily is among the first organizations families turn to. We have absorbed these arrivals in real time, year after year, ahead of any formal plan or funding.

  • Holistic services under one roof

    Crisis financial support, housing, mental health, food security, systems navigation, and community connection are delivered together. A family is met as a whole person rather than handed a list of referrals.

  • Nearly four decades of relationships

    IslamicFamily has served Edmonton's Muslim and newcomer communities since 1986, building exactly the depth of trust and local knowledge that a coordinated emergency response depends on.

Anchor partners

These organizations are trusted by the very communities that arrive in crisis. Today they step in reactively, informally, and without resourcing. This proposal formalizes and funds a role they already play.

Confirmed anchor

IslamicFamily

Nearly four decades anchoring Edmonton's Muslim and newcomer communities across crisis, housing, mental health, and holistic social supports.

Second seat, to be confirmed

One of three shortlisted below

Chosen to complement IslamicFamily's reach so the two anchors together span the widest possible set of communities.

Shortlist for the second staffed anchor

  • Bent Arrow Traditional Healing Society

    Indigenous-led on Treaty 6. Northern First Nations are among Alberta's most frequently evacuated communities, with Edmonton a common reception city, so Bent Arrow reaches a population the current system serves least well.

  • Africa Centre

    A hub for African and Black communities across settlement, connection, and social services, extending reach into communities IslamicFamily does not primarily serve.

  • Newcomer Centre

    Formerly the Edmonton Mennonite Centre for Newcomers, among the largest settlement agencies in the city, with significant service infrastructure and a documented role in the 2015 to 2017 Syrian response.

11

A phased rollout

The model is built to prove itself first, then grow. We stand up the coordination scaffolding with two anchor agencies, test it against real activation, and only then onboard additional partners so growth rests on a foundation that works.

Phase 1
Months 1–4

Build the scaffolding

Hire the four roles, establish the coordination function within EFRS, formalize the civil society seat, set metric baselines, and begin mapping community assets, languages, and services across the city.

Phase 2
Months 5–14

Run the pilot

Embed the two anchor agencies, build the shared community connection map, establish culturally grounded mental health and navigation pathways, and test coordination against real or simulated activation.

Phase 3
Months 15–26

Onboard 2 to 3 more agencies

With the scaffolding proven, extend embedded partnership to two or three additional agencies, broadening cultural and geographic coverage so more communities are reached before the next disaster.

Phase 4
Months 27–36

Evaluate and scale

Independent evaluation against the metrics below, refinement of the model, and a costed case for sustained funding and adoption across other Alberta communities.

12

How we will measure success

Baselines are established in Phase 1 and targets agreed with AEMA, so the pilot can be reported on honestly. Six measures track whether coordination is actually improving outcomes.

01

Time to mobilize

Hours from activation to community anchors deployed and receiving evacuees.

02

Access to appropriate support

Share of evacuees connected to language- and culture-matched supports, especially mental health.

03

Asset-map coverage

Share of key communities and languages with a mapped anchor and a current service inventory.

04

Duplication reduced, gaps owned

Duplicated services counted down; critical functions like navigation given a clear owner.

05

Community offers converted

Share of volunteer and in-kind offers actually deployed rather than lost.

06

Families reached

Families served through the coordinated pathway per activation.

Why now, and why us

The next evacuation is not a question of if. Alberta will see more wildfire and flood displacement, and Edmonton will continue to receive people fleeing crises far beyond our borders. Each time, we rebuild the same informal coordination from scratch, and each time the same families fall through the same gaps.

Edmonton Fire Rescue Services is trusted to lead in emergencies, and coordination is what it does. IslamicFamily is trusted by the communities who arrive, and holistic community support is what it does. Together we are asking for something modest and well precedented: four people, a defined three-year pilot, and a formal seat at the table for the communities who are always there anyway. In return, the Province operationalizes commitments its own Emergency Social Services Framework already makes, and gets a response system that is more efficient, more integrated, and measurably better for people in crisis.

Proposed next step

A 45-minute scoping session with your office and the Alberta Emergency Management Agency to define the pilot scope, confirm metric baselines, and cost the function within the Emergency Social Services Framework.
